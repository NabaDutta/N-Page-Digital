How to Increase Google Reviews for Your Business

No fake reviews, no shortcuts — just what actually works

Most businesses lose reviews not because customers are unhappy, but because nobody actually asked. A satisfied customer rarely thinks to leave a review unprompted.

A simple, direct ask right after a good experience is still the single most effective thing a business can do, and it costs nothing beyond a moment of attention.

Ask at the actual moment of satisfaction

The best time to ask is right after a genuinely good experience — right after a service is completed, a delivery arrives, or a project wraps up.

Not weeks later in a generic follow-up email, by which point the specific, fresh satisfaction that would have prompted a review has usually faded.

Timing matters more than the wording of the ask itself. A well-written request sent at the wrong moment underperforms a plain, simple request sent at exactly the right one.

Make the actual link as easy as possible

A direct link that opens straight to your review box removes the biggest source of drop-off between someone agreeing to leave a review and actually finishing it.

A link to your general profile that makes someone hunt for the review button loses people at exactly the moment their willingness was highest.

This is a five-minute setup, once, that meaningfully increases how many people who say yes actually follow through to a finished review.

Different ways to actually ask

In person, right after a service, works well for businesses with direct customer contact — a simple verbal request, followed up with a text containing the direct link.

A text message sent within an hour of the interaction tends to outperform email, since it's read almost immediately rather than sitting in an inbox.

A QR code at the point of sale or on a printed receipt gives customers who prefer to act on their own time an easy, low-pressure option.

Whichever method fits your business, consistency matters more than picking the theoretically perfect channel — the ask that actually happens beats the ideal one that doesn't.

Never buy or fake reviews

Beyond being against Google's policies, with a real risk of profile suspension, fake reviews are usually detectable in practice.

Oddly generic language, unnatural timing clusters, and reviewer accounts with no other history are exactly the patterns Google's own detection systems are built to catch.

It's not worth the risk for a signal that real customers can build for free, simply by being asked at the right moment.

Respond to every review, not just the positive ones

A thoughtful response to a negative review, addressing the actual concern honestly, often does more for trust than the negative review itself does damage.

Anyone reading it later sees a business that takes feedback seriously, rather than one that either ignores criticism or gets defensive in public.

Positive reviews deserve a genuine reply too, even a short one — it signals an actively managed business, not just a passive collector of star ratings.

Handling a review that shouldn't be there

Occasionally a review violates a platform's actual policies — it's spam, entirely off-topic, or clearly posted by a competitor rather than a real customer.

These can be flagged for removal through the platform's own reporting tools, though removal isn't guaranteed and can take real time to process once submitted.

A genuinely negative but honest review from a real customer is a different situation entirely, and isn't something to try to get removed — it's something to respond to well instead.

Why review volume matters beyond the star rating

The star rating gets most of the attention, but the total number of reviews is itself a separate, meaningful trust and ranking signal.

A business with 4.3 stars and 200 reviews often outperforms one with a perfect 5.0 rating and only 6 reviews, since volume signals a real, established pattern rather than a handful of isolated experiences.

This is part of why a steady stream of new reviews matters more than chasing a marginally higher average once a business already has a reasonably solid rating.

A note on review gating

Some businesses try to filter who gets asked — steering only clearly happy customers toward the public review link, and routing anyone who seems unsatisfied to a private feedback form instead.

This practice, often called review gating, violates the policies of most major review platforms, including Google's, and can result in real penalties if detected.

The honest approach — asking everyone consistently, and handling negative feedback through a good public response rather than by hiding it from the process — holds up better over time and carries none of that risk.

Where reviews fit alongside your Google Business Profile

Reviews are one part of a much larger profile, and their effect compounds when the rest of that profile is also genuinely complete and active.

A steady stream of new reviews on an otherwise neglected profile — wrong hours, no photos, no posts — still helps, but far less than the same reviews on a profile that's clearly well maintained elsewhere too.

Treating review generation as one piece of a broader, ongoing local SEO habit tends to outperform treating it as a standalone task disconnected from everything else.

A simple habit that sustains this long-term

Pick one specific, repeatable moment in your actual customer process — right after checkout, right after a service call ends — rather than leaving the timing to chance each time.

Track roughly how many requests go out each week against how many reviews actually land, so a slowdown gets noticed early rather than months later.

None of this requires special software or a formal system. A simple habit, followed consistently, outperforms an elaborate process that's too complicated to actually maintain.

Setting realistic expectations for how many reviews to expect

Not every satisfied customer will leave a review, even with a direct, well-timed ask — a realistic response rate is a meaningful fraction of people asked, not close to all of them.

This is normal and doesn't mean the ask isn't working. Consistency over months matters more than any single week's conversion rate, and small, steady gains compound over a year far more than they seem to week by week.

What to do when reviews slow down

A drop in new reviews is usually a process problem, not a satisfaction problem — check first whether the ask is still actually happening consistently, before assuming customer sentiment has changed.

Staff turnover is a common, quiet cause: a new team member who was never trained to make the ask simply doesn't, and the gap isn't noticed until someone reviews the numbers weeks or months later.

Revisiting the actual request template and timing occasionally — testing a slightly different moment or wording — can also reveal whether the original approach has quietly stopped resonating with customers.

A seasonal dip is sometimes just that — seasonal — rather than a sign anything is genuinely broken, so it's worth comparing against the same period a year earlier before assuming a real problem exists.

Make it part of your actual process, not an afterthought

A review request that depends on someone remembering to send it manually stops happening within a few weeks, once the initial enthusiasm for the idea fades.

Building the ask into your actual workflow — a line in the invoice template, a message template ready to copy and send — is what keeps it consistent long-term.

Training anyone customer-facing to make the ask naturally, as a normal closing step rather than an awkward extra request, keeps this running without needing constant reminders from ownership.

Frequently asked questions

Can I offer a discount in exchange for a review?
No — Google's policies prohibit incentivized reviews, and this can put your listing at risk. Ask genuinely, without an attached reward.
How do I handle an unfair or fake negative review?
You can flag it for Google's review through your Business Profile dashboard, though removal isn't guaranteed. A calm, factual public response is usually the better immediate move.
How many reviews do I actually need?
There's no fixed number, but review volume itself is a trust signal — a steadily growing count matters more than hitting any specific milestone.
Should I respond to positive reviews too?
Yes — even a short, genuine thank-you shows an actively managed business, which itself is a signal worth building.
